# Can Verse duplicate board games? (US first, EU/UK note)

> **Not legal advice.** This is research for building, not a lawyer's opinion. Talk to an IP lawyer before selling any of this at commercial scale.

## 1. The plain answer

**No to "outright duplicate", yes to "play the same game".** In the US you may copy how a game *plays*: rules as gameplay, mechanics, scoring, turn order, board layout as function. Copyright doesn't cover those. You may **not** copy what it *looks and reads like*: rulebook wording, art, board graphics, card text, sculpted figures. You also can't use names or logos that make people think it's the original. Calling it "digital documentation" changes nothing. A full playable copy is not fair use and not archiving. Games that are truly old (chess, Go, Pachisi, The Landlord's Game, anything published before 1931 whose copyright has run out) can be copied in full, original art included.

## 2. The law, with citations

- **Mechanics and ideas aren't copyrighted.** 17 U.S.C. §102(b) excludes any "idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation". *Baker v. Selden*, 101 U.S. 99 (1879): the system belongs to everyone; only the author's way of explaining it is protected. The Copyright Office's factsheet **FL-108 "Games"** says copyright "does not protect the idea for a game, its name or title, or the method or methods for playing it". It protects only the literary text of the rules and the art or pictures.
- **Names, titles, short phrases.** 37 C.F.R. §202.1(a): words, short phrases, names, titles and slogans can't be copyrighted. §202.1(b) excludes ideas, plans, methods and systems. Names are protected by **trademark**, not copyright.
- **Rules can be reused, as long as you rewrite them.** *Allen v. Academic Games League of America*, 89 F.3d 614 (9th Cir. 1996): game ideas and abstract rules aren't protectable. Only the specific expression is.
- **Copying the expressive layer infringes.** *DaVinci Editrice v. Ziko Games*, 183 F. Supp. 3d 820 (S.D. Tex. 2016), about *Bang!*: the court refused to dismiss, holding that characters, their interplay and how the elements are expressed can be protectable even when the mechanics are free.
- **The look-alike line.** *Tetris Holding v. Xio Interactive*, 863 F. Supp. 2d 394 (D.N.J. 2012): Xio copied Tetris's rules, which is legal, but also its look (piece colours and shapes, board dimensions, visual trade dress), and lost on copyright **and** trade dress. *Spry Fox v. LOLApps/6Waves* (W.D. Wash. 2012): *Yeti Town* reskinned *Triple Town* with the same feel and structure. The court refused to dismiss, and the case settled. **The lesson: identical gameplay is fine, but a near-identical look and feel is not.**
- **Facts and trivia.** *Worth v. Selchow & Righter*, 827 F.2d 569 (9th Cir. 1987): facts aren't owned, so Trivial Pursuit questions drawn from public facts were not infringement. But *Castle Rock v. Carol Publishing*, 150 F.3d 132 (2d Cir. 1998): a trivia book built from *Seinfeld*'s creative content did infringe. Write your own questions from facts.
- **Trademark.** Lanham Act 15 U.S.C. §§1114 and 1125(a): no names, logos or packaging that cause confusion. Surprise: in *Anti-Monopoly v. General Mills Fun Group*, 684 F.2d 1316 (9th Cir. 1982), MONOPOLY was found generic, and Congress then reversed that result in the 1984 Trademark Clarification Act. The mark is valid; don't use it.
- **Trade dress.** *Two Pesos v. Taco Cabana*, 505 U.S. 763 (1992). *Wal-Mart v. Samara*, 529 U.S. 205 (2000): product design needs secondary meaning. *TrafFix v. Marketing Displays*, 532 U.S. 23 (2001): functional features can't be trade dress. A Risk-like map is functional. Risk's exact palette, box and piece silhouettes might not be.
- **Patents.** A utility patent lasts 20 years from filing (35 U.S.C. §154). A design patent lasts 15 years from grant if filed after May 13, 2015, 14 years if filed earlier (35 U.S.C. §173). Every patent on the classics below has expired. Check newer games (2005 and later) on Google Patents. Magie's Landlord's Game patents, **US 748,626 (1904)** and **US 1,509,312 (1924)**, and Darrow's Monopoly patent **US 2,026,082 (1935)** are all expired.
- **Fair use and "documentation".** 17 U.S.C. §107. *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023): a commercial substitute serving the same purpose weighs against fair use. *Hachette v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir. 2024): full digital copies "for archival/lending" were **not** fair use. Reviews, rules commentary and short art excerpts in an article are fine. A playable full reproduction fails.
- **DMCA.** §1201: don't break DRM on digital editions. The library preservation exemptions in 37 C.F.R. §201.40 don't cover public play. §1202: don't strip copyright notices from copied material. §512: once users upload content, register a DMCA agent and honour takedowns.
- **Public domain.** In the US, works published before 1931 are public domain as of Jan 1, 2026 (§304; the 95-year term). Later works may also be public domain if their copyright was never renewed, but verify that. Truly ancient games have no owner at all.
- **EU/UK.** *SAS Institute v. World Programming*, C-406/10 (CJEU 2012): functionality and methods aren't protected. UK: *Nova Productions v. Mazooma Games* [2007] EWCA Civ 219: game rules and ideas aren't protected; the look of each frame and art is. Copyright lasts life plus 70 years, so the clock runs from the author's death, not the publication date. Pre-1931 US public domain may not be public domain in the EU or UK. Registered designs last up to 25 years. Unregistered designs: EU 3 years, UK 10 to 15 years (CDPA 1988 Part III). Trademarks work much like the US.

## 3. Parody law

- **Copyright.** *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994): a parody that **comments on the original** gets more fair-use room, even when it's commercial. But it may borrow only what it needs to "conjure up" the original. A reskin that simply plays the same game and comments on nothing is not parody under *Campbell* or *Warhol*. It's a substitute.
- **Trademark.** *Jack Daniel's Properties v. VIP Products*, 599 U.S. 140 (2023): when a parody uses someone else's mark **as its own brand name or logo**, there's no special First Amendment shield. The ordinary likelihood-of-confusion test applies, and parody only helps to the extent it makes confusion less likely. So "Monopoly-but-Verse" is risky as a product name.
- **The Verse parody recipe:** (1) keep the mechanics, which are free anyway; (2) use 100% original Verse art, names, rules wording, sculpts and palette; (3) make the joke clear and Verse's own (cosmic, glitch, void-robot); (4) no original logos, fonts, box design or confusable names; (5) a "not affiliated with X" line is good hygiene but doesn't cure a confusing name. Legally the parody is the theme. The mechanics are what you're actually allowed to take.

## 4. Rulebook for Verse

**Can be identical:** mechanics, turn structure, scoring, win conditions, board topology as function (number of spaces, grid size, a map of real-world regions), standard components (d6 dice, a 52-card deck, pawns, grids, timers), and public-domain games **in full**, including original public-domain art (scan it from a pre-1931 printing, not from a modern reissue that adds new art).

**Must be original:** rules text wording (rewrite from scratch, don't paraphrase line by line), all art, board graphics, iconography, card text and flavour, character names, the game name, logos, fonts, box design, distinctive sculpts (the Monopoly tokens, Operation's patient, Mr. Potato-style figures), trivia questions, and word or category lists where they were chosen creatively.

**How close a look-alike can get (the Tetris v. Xio lesson):** an observer should never mistake it for the original. Change the palette, piece shapes and silhouettes, board art, layout styling, sounds and UI chrome. Keep only what's functionally needed. Test: put a screenshot next to the original. If a casual player thinks they're the same product, redesign it.

## 6. Top surprises

1. **Monopoly's ancestor is free.** The Landlord's Game patents (1904/1924) and Darrow's 1935 patent are expired, and the 1904 material is PD.
2. **MONOPOLY was once ruled generic** (*Anti-Monopoly*, 1982), until Congress reversed the result in 1984.
3. **About half this list is a trademarked skin on a PD game:** Othello (Reversi), Stratego (L'Attaque), Uno (Crazy Eights), Yahtzee (Yacht), Chutes and Ladders, Mastermind, Sorry!/Trouble (Pachisi), Battleship, Connect Four.
4. **Tetris v. Xio:** clean rules plus the same look still lost on copyright and trade dress.
5. **"Archive" is not a shield.** Internet Archive lost in 2024 over full copies.
6. **The Jack Daniel's case (2023)** means a parody *name* used as a brand gets no free pass.
7. **The EU/UK clock is life+70**, so US pre-1931 PD doesn't automatically carry over to Europe.

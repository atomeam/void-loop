Odd things often seem to come in waves: three strange lights in one week, then nothing for months. But chance alone makes clumps too. Spread events at random over a stretch of time and some land close together while long gaps open up elsewhere. In 1946, R. D. Clarke showed that the flying bombs falling on south London, which people believed were aimed at certain districts, matched a purely random (Poisson) pattern. Seeing patterns in randomness is the "clustering illusion" (Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky, 1985). Paste your own dates below. The page measures how uneven the gaps between them are, then compares that with 2,000 random timelines that have the same number of events over the same span. It cannot say what the events were, only whether their timing is more bunched than chance. Nothing leaves this browser.

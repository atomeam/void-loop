motherfuckingwebsite.com — The One-Page Manifesto

Why it exists: Registered on 22 November 2013 at 02:09:07 UTC, motherfuckingwebsite.com was built as a blunt, hilarious protest against bloated modern web design—megabytes of JavaScript, heavy frameworks, and sluggish layouts just to display a few paragraphs of text.

Music Lore · 1969Unread

Track Times — A Silent Album Track List

Why it exists: Before streaming players added automatic progress bars and album runtime totals, liner notes and fan forums listed raw track timestamps without doing the math for you. Track Times started from a simple itch: paste a tracklist and immediately know how long the full listen takes.

Its classic sample comes from King Crimson's debut album (1969): five tracks in order, running 43 minutes and 54 seconds on the original LP—a reminder of when an album was paced as one continuous side-A and side-B sitting. Try it in the album track time adder.

Web History · 1991Unread

info.cern.ch — The First Web Page

Why it exists: Published on 6 August 1991 by Tim Berners-Lee from a NeXT computer at CERN, the world's first website wasn't a launch announcement or a product pitch—it was a plain-text user manual explaining what the World Wide Web was and how to set up your own server.

No images, no stylesheets, no ads: just blue hyperlinks connecting researchers across labs. Everything we browse today grew out of that single explanatory page.

Rabbit Holes · 1991Unread

The Trojan Room Coffee Pot

Why it exists: In 1991, researchers in Cambridge University's Trojan Room grew tired of walking down several flights of stairs only to find an empty coffee pot. So they pointed a 128x128 grayscale camera at the carafe and streamed snapshots over the local network.

When web browsers gained image support in 1993, they put the feed online—accidentally inventing the world's first webcam and drawing millions of viewers to watch coffee slowly drip in England.

Web History · 1998Unread

HTTP 418: I'm a Teapot

Why it exists: On 1 April 1998, Larry Masinter published RFC 2324, the Hyper Text Coffee Pot Control Protocol (HTCPCP)—an April Fools' joke mocking over-engineered internet protocols and smart-home hype before smart homes even existed.

Status code 418 meant "I'm a teapot" and was returned whenever someone tried to brew coffee with a teapot. Decades later, developers refused to let it be deprecated, keeping 418 alive as a permanent piece of web folklore.

Rabbit Holes · 2005Unread

The Million Dollar Homepage

Why it exists: In August 2005, 21-year-old student Alex Tew needed money for university tuition and decided to sell a 1000x1000 pixel grid on a single webpage for $1 per pixel, in 10x10 blocks.

The stunt snowballed into a chaotic mosaic of early-2000s startups, casinos, and novelty sites, selling out its final pixels on eBay in January 2006 for $1,037,100 gross—a time capsule of mid-2000s web ambition.
